\begin{abstract}
Alignments explain, event by event, how an observed trace deviates from a process model, but their optimality guarantee requires exact shortest-path search over a synchronous product whose state space degrades with concurrency, loops, duplicate labels, and invisible transitions, precisely the ingredients of real discovered models. Purely learned aligners are fast but untrustworthy: their output may be illegal or silently suboptimal. This paper introduces LARA (Learned Adaptive Recombined Alignments), a hybrid system in which a neural model proposes and an exact layer certifies. The neural model is a small foundation model for alignment: trained once, offline, on synthetic data, and vocabulary-free by construction, it applies to any net and any log without per-log training or configuration. Its scores drive a constrained decoder whose candidates are fireable by construction; replay decides legality, and an exact backend certifies optimality by cost equality or repairs the candidate, so the user never receives a wrong alignment, only a fast one or a certified one. On held-out synthetic data, LARA produces legal alignments for 100\% of traces and certified optimal ones for 88\%; a guidance ablation shows the learned contribution is concentrated in duplicate-label resolution (94.4\% versus 66.7\% optimal without guidance). Legality transfers zero-shot to unseen net families and to two real-life event logs, and the learned fast path overtakes exact search at roughly 40 activities on synthetic nets and already on a real discovered model dominated by invisible transitions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining relates recorded event data to process models in order to discover, check, and improve processes \cite{Aalst2016}. Conformance checking quantifies how well observed behavior agrees with modeled behavior \cite{Carmona2018,Rozinat2008}, and alignments are its reference technique \cite{Adriansyah2011,Adriansyah2014}: an alignment explains every event of a trace through synchronous, log, and model moves, and an optimal alignment minimizes the total deviation cost. The result is not merely a fitness number but a diagnostic object showing exactly where log and model disagree.

The guarantee of optimality is also the burden of the technique. Optimal alignments are computed by exact shortest-path search, typically A* \cite{Hart1968} with heuristics derived from the marking equation \cite{Dongen2018,Zelst2018}, over the reachability graph of a synchronous product net. This state space grows quickly with concurrency, loops, duplicate labels, and invisible transitions, and the problem is intrinsically hard \cite{Schwanen2026}. These are exactly the ingredients of the models process discovery produces in practice, so for the analyst the consequence is concrete: on large discovered models, exact alignment is often the bottleneck of the entire analysis, with running times that cannot be predicted from the input size.

Machine learning promises speed, and neural models have been applied successfully to event data for predictive monitoring and discovery \cite{Tax2017,Bukhsh2021,Sommers2021}. However, a purely learned aligner is unusable for conformance checking: a predicted alignment may be \emph{illegal}, meaning its model projection cannot be replayed from the initial to the final marking, or it may be silently suboptimal, and any conformance verdict built on it inherits this uncertainty. The tension is between trust and tractability.

This paper asks whether the tension can be dissolved instead. We present LARA (Learned Adaptive Recombined Alignments), a neural-guided alignment system built around one invariant: \emph{neural output is never trusted as a certificate}. A learned model scores alignment decisions; a constrained greedy decoder turns the scores into a candidate whose model moves are fireable by construction; an exact replay verifier decides legality; and an exact alignment backend remains the sole authority on optimality, certifying the candidate by cost equality or repairing it. Speed comes from the model, but correctness never depends on it: the system degrades from certified optimal to a safe upper bound, never to wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:outline} summarizes the approach.

For the user of conformance checking, this design is interesting for three reasons. First, the neural component is a small \emph{foundation model} for alignment: trained once, offline, on synthetic data, and vocabulary-free through hashed label embeddings, it applies to any event log and any Petri net without retraining or configuration, and every improvement of the shared checkpoint benefits all future analyses. Second, its per-trace latency is dominated by a fixed forward pass, staying flat and predictable where exact search has heavy tails, which is what interactive analysis of large discovered models needs. Third, adopting it is risk-free: every answer is at least a legal alignment with a verified cost, and in certified mode exactly as trustworthy as classical alignment. The contribution decomposes into four parts:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A certifying architecture} strictly separating learned candidate generation from exact legality checking and optimality certification (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
\item \textbf{A vocabulary-free neural model for Petri nets and traces}, with bidirectional arc messages (necessary for duplicate-label disambiguation), cross-attention coupling of net and trace, and hashed labels enabling zero-shot use on unseen vocabularies.
\item \textbf{An exact-labeled synthetic training pipeline} whose traces carry provably optimal alignments at the level of transition identities.
\item \textbf{An evaluation methodology for learned alignment}: a guidance ablation isolating the learned contribution, a scaling benchmark locating the speed and quality threshold against exact A*, and zero-shot validation on real-life logs (Section~\ref{sec:assessment}).
\end{enumerate}

The central question, can a learned model make trace alignment fast without making it less trustworthy, is refined into six research questions. \textbf{RQ1 (legality)}: are the produced alignments always legal, even on unseen nets and vocabularies? \textbf{RQ2 (near-optimality)}: how often is the exact optimal cost attained, and how large are the gaps otherwise? \textbf{RQ3 (ambiguity)}: are duplicate labels and invisible routing resolved? \textbf{RQ4 (generalization)}: does performance transfer across families, scales, and real-life logs? \textbf{RQ5 (certification)}: how often does cost equality certify the candidate? \textbf{RQ6 (attribution)}: how much quality is owed to the learned scores rather than to the constrained search around them?

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}

\textbf{Foundations and alignments.} Petri nets \cite{Murata1989} remain central to conformance checking \cite{Aalst2016,Carmona2018}. Replay-based conformance checking links trace events to model elements \cite{Rozinat2008}; cost-based alignments \cite{Adriansyah2011,Adriansyah2014} introduced the synchronous, log, and model move vocabulary used throughout this paper, and van Zelst et al.\ \cite{Zelst2018} formalize alignment computation as a shortest-path problem over a synchronous product. LARA preserves this diagnostic tradition: its output is an alignment object with transition identities, not a predicted score.

\textbf{Exact search and acceleration.} Efficient exact methods refine A* \cite{Hart1968} with (extended) marking-equation heuristics \cite{Dongen2018} or reduce explored states as in REACH \cite{Casas2024}; complexity analyses underline why the problem resists such optimizations in general \cite{Schwanen2026}. These approaches provide formal guidance inside one search; LARA instead provides empirical guidance outside the exact search and keeps the exact layer for certification only.

\textbf{Decomposition and recomposition.} Decomposing nets speeds up conformance checking \cite{Aalst2013}, local alignments can be merged \cite{Verbeek2016}, recomposition restores exact results or bounds \cite{Lee2018}, and automata with S-components offer another scalable route \cite{Reissner2020}. LARA is architecturally close to this family, but its regions are latent and trace-dependent rather than fixed structural decompositions.

\textbf{Approximate conformance checking.} Token-based replay \cite{Rozinat2008,Berti2021}, alignment approximation for process trees \cite{Schuster2021}, and relaxation labeling \cite{Padro2022} occupy the same speed and accuracy space as LARA's fast mode. The distinctive feature of LARA is the certification boundary: candidates are replay-verified, then certified by cost equality or repaired exactly.
\textbf{Neural methods in process mining.} Neural models of event data have mostly targeted prediction, with recurrent networks \cite{Tax2017}, transformers \cite{Vaswani2017,Bukhsh2021}, and graph neural networks for discovery \cite{Sommers2021}; a recent survey identifies conformance checking as an open opportunity for AI \cite{Genga2025}. None of these works constructs legal, costed alignments against an explicit Petri net; LARA is a concrete instance of that agenda.

\textbf{Learning-augmented search.} Outside process mining, machine learning guides combinatorial optimization \cite{Bengio2021} and shortest-path search, as in Neural A* \cite{Yonetani2021}. LARA is deliberately more conservative: learned scores rank moves for a greedy decoder and are never used as admissible heuristics, so the trust model of exact conformance checking is preserved. The gap LARA addresses is thus \emph{certifiable neural guidance} for Petri net trace alignment.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

\textbf{Events, traces, and logs.} Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite alphabet of activity labels. A trace $\sigma = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_n \rangle \in \mathcal{A}^*$ is a finite sequence of events describing one case; an event log is a multiset of traces. We align a single trace against a model; log-level results follow by aggregation.

\textbf{Labeled Petri nets.} A Petri net is a tuple $N = (P, T, F)$ with places $P$, transitions $T$, $P \cap T = \emptyset$, and flow relation $F \subseteq (P \times T) \cup (T \times P)$. A marking $m : P \to \mathbb{N}$ assigns tokens to places; a transition is enabled iff all its input places carry a token, and firing it consumes from input and produces into output places, written $m \xrightarrow{t} m'$. A labeling function $\lambda : T \to \mathcal{A} \cup \{\tau\}$ marks transitions as visible or invisible ($\tau$); invisible transitions represent routing that leaves no trace in the log, and two distinct transitions may share a visible label (duplicate labels). Both phenomena are central here: an observed event determines the \emph{label} of a matching transition but not its \emph{identity}. A system net $SN = (N, m_0, m_f)$ adds initial and final markings.

\textbf{Alignments.} Let $\gg$ denote a skip symbol. A move is a pair $(x, y) \in (\mathcal{A} \cup \{\gg\}) \times (T \cup \{\gg\})$, excluding $(\gg, \gg)$: a synchronous move $(a, t)$ with $\lambda(t) = a$, a log move $(a, \gg)$, or a model move $(\gg, t)$. An alignment of $\sigma$ and $SN$ is a sequence of moves $\gamma$ such that (1) the sequence of non-$\gg$ log components equals $\sigma$ exactly, and (2) the sequence of non-$\gg$ model components is a firing sequence of $N$ from $m_0$ that reaches $m_f$. An alignment satisfying both conditions is \emph{legal}. Moves carry the transition identity $t$, not just its label: with duplicate labels, two alignments can have identical label projections yet fire different transitions.

\textbf{Costs and optimality.} The standard unit cost model charges $0$ for synchronous moves and invisible model moves and $1$ for log moves and visible model moves. The cost of an alignment is $c(\gamma) = \sum_i c(x_i, y_i)$, and an alignment is optimal iff its cost equals $\delta(\sigma, SN) = \min \{ c(\gamma) \mid \gamma \text{ legal} \}$. Optimal alignments need not be unique; the optimal cost is, and under unit costs it counts the minimum number of deviations needed to explain the trace.

\textbf{Synchronous product and A*.} The classical construction builds a synchronous product of $SN$ and a linear trace net; legal alignments correspond exactly to complete firing sequences of the product, so computing an optimal alignment is a shortest-path problem over its reachability graph \cite{Zelst2018}, solved by A* with admissible heuristics from the (extended) marking equation \cite{Dongen2018}. We use the state-equation A* aligner of PM4Py \cite{Berti2023} as exact backend and as the source of ground-truth labels.

\textbf{Certification.} A certificate of optimality for a candidate $\gamma$ is a proof that $c(\gamma) = \delta(\sigma, SN)$. Since the exact solver returns an optimal cost, cost equality certifies a legal candidate, even if it differs from the exact alignment; this rule is used throughout the paper.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

LARA separates \emph{candidate generation} (learned, fast, uncertified) from \emph{certification} (exact, trusted). Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the pipeline. At alignment time a net and trace pair passes through three stages, and each stage consumes exactly what the previous one produces: input encoding re-encodes the pair for the neural model (Section~\ref{sec:encoding}), the neural model scores the possible moves (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), and the decoder turns the scores into one alignment that exact methods then verify and certify (Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). Each component is introduced through the alignment question it answers. Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:training} describe the offline training of the neural model, which happens once and is amortized over all later use.

\subsection{Input Encoding: Reading the Net and the Trace}
\label{sec:encoding}

\emph{Input:} a system net $SN = (N, m_0, m_f)$ and a trace $\sigma$ of length $n$. \emph{Output:} a graph view of the net, the trace as a sequence of label identifiers, and a compatibility mask $C$ recording which event and transition pairs share a label.

\textbf{The net as a typed graph.} The net becomes a bipartite graph whose nodes carry structural attributes (marking tokens and degrees per place; invisibility, degrees, move costs, and self-loop context per transition). Every arc is materialized in \emph{both} directions, with the direction kept as a type. The reverse direction matters for a genuinely process-oriented reason: what distinguishes two transitions sharing a label is typically \emph{downstream} of them, for example which branch suffix follows each. With messages flowing only along arc direction, two duplicates with symmetric presets would end up with provably identical representations; with bidirectional messages the disambiguating suffix reaches each duplicate in two hops, which is what later lets the model tell duplicates apart (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\textbf{Activity labels without a vocabulary.} Activity labels are mapped to a bounded set of 8{,}192 identifiers by a stable hash (BLAKE2b), one identifier being reserved for $\tau$; the trace becomes its sequence of hashed identifiers. The consequence is central for practice: the model never memorizes activity names, so one trained checkpoint applies unchanged to logs whose vocabularies it has never seen, the prerequisite for using it as a foundation model across event logs (Section~\ref{sec:reallife}). An event and a transition with the same label receive the same identifier, which ties trace positions to their candidate transitions. The boolean mask $C$, with $C_{ij} = 1$ iff event $i$ and transition $j$ carry the same visible label, later guarantees that only label-consistent synchronous moves can be proposed.

\subsection{Neural Scoring: Three Questions per Net and Trace Pair}
\label{sec:architecture}

\emph{Input:} the encoded pair of Section~\ref{sec:encoding}. \emph{Output:} scored answers to the three questions any aligner faces. For every event $i$ and transition $j$: how plausible is it that event $i$ synchronizes with exactly transition $j$ (synchronous scores $\mathrm{sync} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times |T|}$, masked by $C$)? For every event: how plausible is it that it is a deviation to be skipped (log-move scores in $\mathbb{R}^n$)? For every transition: how plausible is it that the model requires it although no event matches (model-move scores in $\mathbb{R}^{|T|}$)? Only these move scores travel further down the pipeline, to the decoder of Section~\ref{sec:decoding}. The model is deliberately small, roughly 3M parameters (hidden dimension $d = 128$), and runs comfortably on CPU.

\textbf{Two coupled encoders.} A graph transformer reads the net: over three rounds, every place and transition refines its representation by attending to all other nodes and exchanging messages with its neighbors, separately per arc type and direction, so structural context accumulates in each node. A sequence transformer \cite{Vaswani2017} reads the trace, embedding events through the \emph{same} label identifiers as transitions, so an event and its label-compatible transitions start from the same representation. One round of bidirectional cross-attention couples the two encoders: every event looks at the transitions that could explain it, and every transition looks at the events that could justify firing it. This coupling makes all scores \emph{trace-dependent}: the same net is scored differently depending on where the trace deviates.

\textbf{Move-scoring heads.} The synchronous score of event $i$ and transition $j$ compares their two representations (a scaled bilinear form), hard-masked by $C$; row $i$ is the model's belief about \emph{which concrete transition} event $i$ should synchronize with, and this is where duplicate-label resolution happens. Log-move and model-move scores are read off the event and transition representations directly.

\textbf{A diagnostic decomposition branch.} A learned router additionally assigns transitions and events softly to $R = 8$ latent regions, and per region a small head predicts a local deviation sketch (out of $S = 6$ patterns), lower and upper cost bounds, and an uncertainty estimate; the summed upper bounds are the model's estimate of the total alignment cost, supervised against the optimum. Figure~\ref{fig:regions} illustrates the branch. It is the learned analogue of decomposition-based conformance checking \cite{Aalst2013,Verbeek2016}: instead of a fixed structural decomposition, the partition is predicted per net and trace pair, so the same net is partitioned differently depending on where the trace deviates. To avoid a misreading: this branch is \emph{diagnostic only}. Its outputs never reach the decoder or the exact search, since a learned cost bound carries no admissibility guarantee.

\textbf{How the decoder uses the scores.} The decoder builds the candidate in a single left-to-right pass over the whole trace while tracking the current marking (Figure~\ref{fig:decoding}); despite the diagnostic region branch, there is no decomposition of the trace and no recombination of partial alignments at this stage. Two of the three score families are consumed, each at one specific decision point. For event $a_i$: (1) among the transitions that are \emph{enabled} in the current marking and labeled $a_i$, the decoder picks the one with the highest synchronous score; the marking already filters the candidates, so the score matters exactly when several enabled transitions share a label, i.e., the synchronous scores implement duplicate-label resolution. (2) If no such transition is enabled, the decoder looks for a short sequence of model moves that enables one: a breadth-first search over markings, bounded at depth 8, whose successors are ordered invisible-first and then by descending model-move score, i.e., the model-move scores decide which routing path through invisible or skipped transitions the model most plausibly took. On success the path is emitted as model moves followed by the synchronous move. (3) If the bounded search also fails, event $a_i$ is emitted as a log move; this fallback needs no learned score, which is why the log-move head serves training and diagnostics rather than decoding. After the last event, a final bounded search (depth at most 32) emits the model moves reaching $m_f$. Since every emitted model move is fired on the tracked marking, the candidate $\gamma$ is fireable by construction.

\textbf{What the bounded search is, and is not.} The breadth-first search is not an A* alignment search in miniature. Exact aligners search the reachability graph of the synchronous product, may defer or revise any decision, and provably return the global cost minimum \cite{Zelst2018,Dongen2018}. The decoder's search instead explores model moves only, starts from the current marking only, stops at the \emph{first} marking enabling the next label rather than the cheapest one, and every commitment is irrevocable: it is a local fireability probe that guarantees the candidate can be replayed, not that it is optimal, which is exactly the failure mode analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:failure} and the reason the exact layer stays in the loop. A precomputed per-net runtime keeps the probe cheap, so the neural forward pass dominates fast-mode wall time.

\textbf{Verifier and certifier.} The verifier receives only $\gamma$: it replays the transition projection from $m_0$, checks that $m_f$ is reached and that the log projection reconstructs $\sigma$ exactly, and computes $c(\gamma)$. The certifier turns the verified candidate into the final result in one of three modes: \textsc{fast} returns it directly, with $c(\gamma)$ as an upper bound on $\delta$; \textsc{certified} additionally runs the exact aligner and either certifies the candidate by cost equality (Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}) or returns the exact alignment as repair; \textsc{anytime} degrades gracefully on exact-backend timeouts, returning the best available bounds.

\subsection{Exact-Labeled Training Data}
\label{sec:data}

The scoring heads of Section~\ref{sec:architecture} are trained offline, once, on synthetic net and trace pairs whose optimal alignments are known exactly. Per example, a model family is sampled: with probability 0.8 a sequence net over 3 to 8 activities (optionally prefixed by an invisible transition), and with probability 0.2 a duplicate-label choice net, an exclusive choice between two branches whose first transitions share a label, followed by distinguishing suffixes and invisible joins; by construction the trace suffix, not the ambiguous event itself, reveals the intended transition. Controlled deviations are injected into a fitting trace (rate 0.25: per-event deletion, insertion, duplication, plus trace-level swap and append). The exact backend labels each pair with a provably optimal alignment, which must also pass replay verification; failures are discarded. Examples are split into train, validation, and test sets (400, 100, 100) stratified jointly by family and optimal-cost bucket. Traces average 5.3 events with optimal costs 0 to 6; every sample stores the optimal alignment \emph{with transition identities} and its exact cost.

\subsection{Training Objective and Procedure}
\label{sec:training}

Given the corpus of Section~\ref{sec:data}, the model is trained to \emph{imitate the exact aligner}: the loss combines imitation of the optimal moves, cost shaping, and router regularization, each term supervising one output of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}:
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L} = w_m (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}) + w_c \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cost}} + w_b \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bound}} + w_\ell \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bal}} + w_e \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ent}}.
\end{equation*}
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}}$ is the cross-entropy of synchronous scores against the \emph{optimal transition identity} at synchronously aligned positions; supervising identities rather than labels is what teaches duplicate-label disambiguation. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}$ are binary cross-entropies against optimal log and model moves, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cost}}$ penalizes deviation of the summed upper bounds from $\delta$ (smooth L1), and the router terms penalize regions that cut shared places, unbalanced loads, and diffuse assignments, with weights $1.0$, $0.03$, $0.01$, $0.01$, $0.001$.

Training uses AdamW with dropout, gradient accumulation, gradient clipping, a plateau scheduler, and early stopping (all hyperparameters in the repository, Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). The reference run took 22 epochs at roughly 32 seconds per epoch on a single CPU, reaching the best validation loss of 0.4614 at epoch 18 (Figure~\ref{fig:panels}, left); the moderate train-validation gap indicates the 3M-parameter model does not yet overfit the 400-example corpus. The hardest component is the log-move head, since deciding which events are deviations is intrinsically ambiguous when random insertions duplicate legitimate labels; the synchronous head converges to a near-zero loss.

\label{sec:assessment}

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:implementation}

LARA is implemented in Python on top of PyTorch and PM4Py \cite{Berti2023} and is available as open source at \url{https://github.com/fit-alessandro-berti/lara-align}. The repository contains the evaluated checkpoint, all result artifacts, and the scripts behind every number in this section, from corpus generation (Section~\ref{sec:data}) and training to the in-distribution, scaling, and real-life evaluations, including a \texttt{--no-guidance} switch for the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. For use on own data, \texttt{scripts/align\_log.py} aligns an XES event log against a PNML net in any of the three modes, and a Python API accepts PM4Py objects and returns the alignment with its cost, bounds, and certification status.

\textbf{Protocol.} All experiments run on a single CPU. The learned method is always evaluated in \emph{fast} mode (verified neural candidate only), so no exact repair contaminates learned metrics; the exact optimum of the PM4Py state-equation A* aligner serves as ground truth for every sample. The metrics instantiate the research questions: the replayable (legal) rate (RQ1), the optimal-cost rate and the cost-gap distribution $c(\gamma) - \delta$ (RQ2), transition-sequence and label-level match rates (RQ3), per-family, scaling, and real-life breakdowns (RQ4), the certified-optimal rate (RQ5), and the guidance ablation (RQ6).

\subsection{Zero-Shot Validation on Real-Life Logs}
\label{sec:reallife}

To test external validity, the same checkpoint was evaluated without any fine-tuning on two real-life logs: the receipt phase of a Dutch municipality's building-permit process \cite{ReceiptLog} (1{,}434 traces, 116 variants, 27 activity labels) and a road traffic fine management sample \cite{RoadTrafficLog} (100 traces, 10 variants, 10 labels). For each log a Petri net is discovered with the inductive miner \cite{Leemans2013} at noise thresholds 0.0 (perfectly fitting, so every optimal cost is 0) and 0.5 (filtered, so rarer variants genuinely deviate). The test is strictly zero-shot: the labels were never seen in training and embed only via the hash, and inductive-miner nets are dominated by invisible routing transitions (47 invisible versus 27 visible for the fitting receipt model), far from the training distribution.

\subsection{Qualitative Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failure}

The largest-gap test cases share one pattern. In the worst case (gap 6), the trace $\langle A, E, B, C, D, E, F, G \rangle$ is aligned against an eight-step sequence net: the optimum skips the two spurious $E$ events (cost 2), whereas LARA fast-forwards through $B$, $C$, $D$ as model moves to synchronize with the early spurious $E$ and must then emit the observed $B$, $C$, $D$ and the second $E$ as log moves (cost 8). The root cause is decoding-time myopia, not representation failure: greedy commitments are irrevocable, and the bounded search cannot see that skipped events reappear later, exactly the limitation anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:decoding}. This motivates neural-guided beam or A* search, where the same learned scores rank paths rather than single moves.

\textbf{Summary against the research questions.} Candidates are always legal across all three tested distributions (RQ1); 88\% attain the exact optimum, with even gaps only (RQ2); duplicate-label identity resolution is essentially solved at training scale, and it is specifically the learned component that solves it (RQ3, RQ6); legality generalizes everywhere, while optimality generalizes to real frequent behavior but not yet to large stress nets, making broader training data the clear next step (RQ4); and 88\% of candidates are certified by a single exact call, about 1 ms per trace at training scale (RQ5).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Implications.} The results validate the central design bet: trust and tractability need not be traded off if the learned and exact components are given the right roles. Legality is delivered by construction and verification, independent of distribution shift, and quality degrades into certified-or-repaired territory instead of silent error. The ablation methodology matters beyond this system: any learned aligner wrapped around a constrained decoder should demonstrate what learning adds, because constrained search alone is a strong baseline.

\textbf{Limitations.} The synthetic training families are small (3 to 9 transitions), which the scaling benchmark exposes directly: quality at 40 activities drops to about 30\% optimal because nothing similar was ever trained on. The greedy decoder's myopia produces the even-gap detours of Section~\ref{sec:failure}. Larger log collections, GPU batching, and comparisons against further approximate aligners \cite{Schuster2021,Padro2022,Reissner2020} remain open, as do data-aware, declarative, and object-centric settings \cite{DeLeoni2013,Aalst2020ocpn}.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} Internally, guided and unguided runs share generator seeds and inputs, and all quality metrics are computed against exact optima; scaling configurations use only 10 samples, so per-cell differences carry sampling noise. Externally, two logs cannot establish generality, although the zero-shot protocol makes the transfer test strict. Construct validity rests on cost equality as the certification rule, sound because the exact backend returns provably optimal costs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced LARA, a neural-guided and certifying approach to Petri net trace alignment in which learning proposes and exact reasoning certifies. A vocabulary-free neural model scores alignment moves, a constrained greedy decoder emits a candidate that is fireable by construction, and replay verification plus an exact backend decide legality and optimality, so the system is never less trustworthy than classical conformance checking. Because the model is trained once, offline, and is independent of activity vocabularies, it acts as a small foundation model for conformance checking: no per-log training, flat and predictable latency, and answers that are certified, repaired, or explicitly bounded. Empirically, the prototype produces legal alignments for every trace across three distributions, including zero-shot transfer to real-life logs; it attains and certifies the exact optimum for 88\% of in-distribution traces; its learned component measurably solves duplicate-label identity resolution (94.4\% versus 66.7\% unguided); and its fast path overtakes exact search at roughly 40 activities and already on a real discovered model dominated by invisible transitions.

The remaining gap is quality at scale, and the assessment turns it into a falsifiable target: training on the existing block-structured generator must lift the guided curve above the unguided baseline established here. Beyond that, replacing greedy decoding by neural-guided beam or A* search attacks the even-gap detours at their root, and multi-perspective, data-aware, and object-centric alignment settings offer natural extensions of the certifying architecture.
