\begin{abstract}
Alignments explain how observed behavior deviates from a process model, but computing an optimum requires exact search and can make interactive conformance checking unpredictable. Learned guidance offers predictable candidate generation, yet a neural prediction may be illegal or silently suboptimal. LARA (Learned Alignment with Replay Assurance) addresses this tension through a strict separation of roles. A vocabulary-independent neural scorer proposes moves for previously unseen net and trace pairs, a marking-aware decoder constructs a candidate, replay checks its legality and cost, and an exact backend remains the sole authority for certification and repair. The evaluation shows that the semantic wrapper consistently yields legal candidates, learned guidance is most useful for duplicate-label ambiguity and identifier-stable decisions, and fast candidate generation can outperform exact search on harder tested models, although established approximations generally return better candidates. LARA therefore contributes a reusable, progressive alignment architecture that combines learned proposals with an explicit semantic trust boundary rather than replacing exact or classical alignment methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining relates event data to process models \cite{Aalst2016}; conformance checking compares their behavior, and alignments explain each event through synchronous, log, and model moves \cite{Adriansyah2011}. An optimal alignment minimizes deviation cost, yielding both a fitness value and a diagnostic account of disagreement. Computing it requires shortest-path search over a synchronous product, commonly guided by marking-equation heuristics \cite{Dongen2018,Zelst2018}. The state space grows with concurrency, loops, duplicate labels, and invisible transitions, making exact alignment intrinsically hard \cite{Schwanen2026}. These constructs are common in discovered models, so one difficult variant can dominate log-level running time and make interactive analysis unpredictable.

Learned guidance can provide low-latency candidates, but a prediction may be illegal or silently suboptimal. Either failure is consequential: an illegal sequence is not an explanation of the observed case, while an unreported cost gap distorts fitness and downstream diagnostics. LARA (Learned Alignment with Replay Assurance) therefore enforces one invariant: \emph{neural output is never a certificate}. A neural model scores moves; a marking-aware greedy decoder constructs a candidate; replay verifies legality and cost; and an exact backend certifies it by cost equality or repairs it. Thus learning proposes, while process semantics supplies every guarantee.

The neural component is a reusable, topology- and trace-conditioned move scorer. It is trained once on exact-labeled synthetic data and accepts unseen labels and Petri nets without retraining under the stated system-net and unit-cost setting. Its latency is dominated by feature construction and one forward pass, while exact search may be heavy-tailed. The contributions are (1) this vocabulary-independent scorer; (2) a trust architecture that separates learned proposal, replay verification, and exact proof (Section~\ref{sec:approach}); (3) an exact-labeled synthetic pipeline with transition-identity supervision; and (4) an assessment that isolates the 4.1-point learned contribution, locates the scaling threshold, compares established approximations, and tests transfer (Section~\ref{sec:assessment}).

We ask: \textbf{RQ1, candidate contract and quality:} are returned candidates legal, and what are their optimality rates and gaps? \textbf{RQ2, contribution of learning:} when do learned scores improve the same semantics-constrained decoder? \textbf{RQ3, transfer and efficiency:} how do quality and latency change across representations, scales, real logs, and competing methods?

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}

\textbf{Exact and decomposed alignment.} Alignments are shortest paths through a synchronous product \cite{Zelst2018}; marking-equation heuristics provide admissible lower bounds and accelerate exact search \cite{Dongen2018}, but worst-case complexity remains high \cite{Schwanen2026}. Structural decomposition reduces the state space by aligning fragments, after which recomposition restores a global result or bound \cite{Aalst2013,Lee2018}. Both families derive their guarantees from the model. LARA retains that principle but changes the division of labor: its neural scorer predicts trace-dependent move preferences, while replay and exact search alone establish claims about the returned alignment.

\textbf{Approximation.} Discounted A* changes the cost function \cite{Boltenhagen2021}; fixed-horizon search commits bounded prefixes \cite{Dongen2017}; tandem repeats compress traces \cite{ReissnerTandem2020}; sliding windows retain promising boundary markings \cite{Bogdanov2024}; and subset selection transfers representative alignments by edit distance \cite{FaniSani2020}. These methods exploit different regularities and therefore have different failure surfaces: bounded prefixes and windows lose global context, compression depends on repetition, and subset transfer depends on representative traces. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares their PM4Py implementations under independent replay and common unit costs. LARA differs less in output format than in trust handling: replay checks every candidate and exposes a cost only when it is legal, after which exact cost equality certifies it or exact search repairs it.

\textbf{Learning.} Neural process models largely target prediction \cite{Bukhsh2021}, and a recent survey identifies conformance checking as an open AI opportunity \cite{Genga2025}. Predicting the next activity or a scalar fitness value does not provide the move-by-move explanation required here. LARA instead constructs an alignment against an explicit Petri net and retains concrete transition identities. Its learned scores guide a constrained decoder but never act as admissible heuristics or certificates. This conservative interface makes distribution shift observable as a larger candidate gap or an exact repair, rather than silently weakening the semantics of conformance checking.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

\textbf{Traces and nets.} Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite activity alphabet and $\sigma = \langle a_1,\ldots,a_n\rangle\in\mathcal{A}^*$ a trace; a log is a multiset of traces. A Petri net is $N=(P,T,F)$, where $P$ and $T$ are disjoint sets of places and transitions and $F\subseteq(P\times T)\cup(T\times P)$ is the flow relation. A marking $m:P\to\mathbb{N}$ assigns tokens to places. Transition $t$ is enabled when its input places contain the required tokens; firing consumes these tokens and produces tokens in its output places, written $m\xrightarrow{t}m'$. The labeling $\lambda:T\to\mathcal{A}\cup\{\tau\}$ permits invisible routing ($\tau$) and duplicate visible labels. These constructs matter because an event identifies a matching label but not necessarily a transition identity. A system net $SN=(N,m_0,m_f)$ adds initial and final markings; we assume at least one firing sequence from $m_0$ to $m_f$ exists.

\textbf{Alignments and cost.} With skip symbol $\gg$, a move is synchronous $(a,t)$ when $\lambda(t)=a$, log $(a,\gg)$, or model $(\gg,t)$; $(\gg,\gg)$ is excluded. An alignment $\gamma$ is \emph{legal} iff (1) its non-skip log projection equals $\sigma$ exactly and (2) its model projection is a firing sequence from $m_0$ to $m_f$. Moves retain transition identities, so equal label projections may still choose different duplicate transitions and induce different future behavior. Under unit costs, synchronous and invisible model moves cost $0$, while log and visible model moves cost $1$. Thus
\[
  \delta(\sigma,SN)=\min\{c(\gamma)\mid \gamma\text{ is legal for }\sigma\text{ and }SN\}
\]
is the minimum number of visible deviations required to explain the trace. Optimal alignments need not be unique, but their cost is.

\textbf{Exact search and certification.} The classical construction combines $SN$ with a linear trace net. Legal alignments correspond to complete firing sequences of this synchronous product, making optimal alignment a shortest-path problem \cite{Zelst2018}; A* uses admissible lower bounds derived from the marking equation \cite{Dongen2018}. We use PM4Py's state-equation A* aligner \cite{Berti2023} as exact backend and teacher. For a replay-verified candidate, equality with the exact cost $\delta$ certifies optimality even when the move sequence differs. The current certified mode obtains $\delta$ by running this backend for every trace; using the candidate as an incumbent to reduce exact work remains future work.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

LARA separates learned, fast candidate generation from exact, trusted certification. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} follows a net--trace pair through encoding, neural move scoring, marking-aware decoding, replay, and exact certification. Training is offline and amortized over later use.


\subsection{Input Encoding: Reading the Net and the Trace}
\label{sec:encoding}

\emph{Input:} $SN=(N,m_0,m_f)$ and trace $\sigma$ of length $n$. \emph{Output:} a typed net graph, hashed trace labels, and compatibility mask $C$.

\textbf{Typed graph.} The net becomes a bipartite graph with one node per place and transition. Places carry initial/final token counts and degrees, exposing where execution starts and ends and where branches synchronize. Transitions carry invisibility, move costs, degrees, and a self-loop flag, exposing routing, choice, and repeatable behavior. Each arc becomes two typed directed edges: the type distinguishes presets, which enable a transition, from postsets, which receive its tokens, while the reverse edge lets downstream context flow back toward a choice. This is essential for duplicate labels. If two transitions have symmetric presets but different suffixes, forward-only messages leave them indistinguishable; bidirectional messages bring that suffix evidence to each duplicate. The tokenized representation is linear in net size and avoids the potentially exponential reachability graph, but dense net and trace self-attention are quadratic in their token counts and cross-attention is bilinear.

\textbf{Vocabulary-free labels.} Stable BLAKE2b hashing maps labels to 8{,}192 identifiers, reserving one for $\tau$. Equal event and transition labels share an identifier, so they begin from the same embedding without requiring a global activity dictionary. Hashing provides a fixed-size interface, while random label remapping during training discourages name memorization. Collisions can share an embedding but cannot create an illegal synchronous move because the compatibility mask uses exact label equality: $C_{ij}=1$ precisely when event $i$ and transition $j$ carry the same visible label.

\subsection{Neural Move Scoring}
\label{sec:architecture}

\emph{Input:} the encoded pair. \emph{Output:} synchronous scores $\mathrm{sync}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|T|}$ (masked by $C$) and model-move scores in $\mathbb{R}^{|T|}$. The model has about 1.7M parameters ($d=96$) and runs on CPU.

\textbf{Coupled encoders.} A two-layer graph transformer exchanges typed messages on the net, combining global self-attention with local arc-direction-specific updates. A sequence transformer \cite{Vaswani2017} reads the trace, adds positions, and uses the same label embeddings as the transition nodes. Bidirectional cross-attention then lets events inspect the transitions that could explain them and lets transitions inspect the events that could justify firing them. Consequently, the same net receives different move scores for different traces: the representation is structural, but the decisions are trace-dependent.

\textbf{Scoring heads.} A scaled bilinear head scores event--transition pairs and applies $C$ as a hard mask. A row therefore represents a distribution over concrete transition identities, not merely activity labels, and can resolve duplicates that lead to different suffixes. A linear model-move head orders catch-up paths. The checkpoint also predicts a log-move score for each event, but the current decoder does not consume it: a log move is emitted only when bounded catch-up fails. The log head is therefore an auxiliary training objective, and jointly ranking it against catch-up candidates is an untested extension motivated by Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

The checkpoint additionally exposes experimental localization and cost-interval endpoints. They are neither calibrated nor valid bounds and affect neither decoding nor certification; architecture and calibration details are provided with the artifact.

\subsection{Decoding, Verification, and Certification}
\label{sec:decoding}

\emph{Input:} the net, trace, and move scores. \emph{Output:} a replay-verified upper bound, a certified optimum, or an exact repair. Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} gives the procedure and Figure~\ref{fig:decoding} an example. Scores affect only decoding; replay and exact search inspect the resulting alignment, never neural output.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Guided greedy decoding, verification, and certification.}
\label{alg:decode}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require system net $SN = (N, m_0, m_f)$, trace $\sigma = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_n \rangle$, move scores of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, mode
\State $\gamma \gets \langle\,\rangle$;\quad $m \gets m_0$ \Comment{every appended model component is fired on $m$}
\For{$i = 1, \ldots, n$}
  \State $E \gets \{\, t \text{ enabled at } m \mid \lambda(t) = a_i \,\}$
  \If{$|E| = 1$} append the synchronous move $(a_i, t)$ \Comment{forced by the marking}
  \ElsIf{$|E| > 1$} append $(a_i, t^\ast)$ with $t^\ast = \arg\max_{t \in E} \mathrm{sync}(i, t)$ \Comment{duplicate labels}
  \Else{} BFS over model moves from $m$, depth $\leq 8$: invisible transitions first, then by model-move score
    \If{the search reaches a marking enabling $a_i$} append its model moves, then the synchronous move
    \Else{} append the log move $(a_i, \gg)$ \Comment{bounded catch-up failed}
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State bounded search for model path $\pi_f$ from $m$ to $m_f$, depth $\leq 32$; append $\pi_f$ if found
\State \emph{verify}: replay $\gamma$ from $m_0$; check that $m_f$ is reached and the log projection equals $\sigma$
\If{$\pi_f$ was not found or replay fails} \Return no verified bound in \textsc{fast}, or an exact alignment in \textsc{certified}
\EndIf
\State compute $c(\gamma)$
\If{mode is \textsc{fast}} \Return $\gamma$ with the verified upper bound $c(\gamma) \geq \delta(\sigma, SN)$
\Else{} compute $\delta$ by exact A*; \Return $\gamma$ certified if $c(\gamma) = \delta$, else the exact alignment as repair
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The decoder walks over the trace once and always knows the current marking, so every appended model component is fireable. A unique enabled label match synchronizes without consulting the network; if no short model-move path can enable the event, the decoder emits a log move. Learned synchronous scores settle choices between enabled transitions with the same label, where the trace suffix can distinguish branches that a structural tie-break cannot; model-move scores order catch-up routes. The log-move head, diagnostic intervals, and regions do not participate. A bounded completion can still fail to reach $m_f$, so replay is a rejection gate rather than a claim that decoding is legal by construction. No such rejection occurred in the evaluated instances.

This design differs fundamentally from exact alignment. The bounded searches stop at the first enabling marking rather than the cheapest globally compatible one and never revise an earlier commitment. Replay independently checks both projections and computes $c(\gamma)$. \textsc{Fast} returns that verified upper bound. \textsc{Certified} still invokes exact A* for every trace, then retains the candidate if $c(\gamma)=\delta$ or returns the exact alignment as repair. Thus the 91.0\% rate reported below counts candidates confirmed and retained, not cases that avoid exact search. A precomputed per-net runtime keeps local searches cheap, so fast-mode latency is dominated by encoding and the forward pass. Irrevocable commitments and the unused log score motivate the extensions in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Training Data Generation}
\label{sec:data}

The sampling unit is a \emph{behavior family}: one visible behavior, two Petri-net representations, and two observed traces shared by both nets, hence four rows. Table~\ref{tab:motifs} defines the four equally weighted motifs. The three controlled motifs receive the same two fresh sequential activities after the displayed core, making traces less degenerate without changing the paired comparison.

\textbf{Ordinary tree and canonical block.} ``Ordinary'' names the heterogeneous random class, not a Petri net whose graph is a tree. A process tree has 4--12 visible leaves, depth at most six, and sequence/XOR/AND operators sampled with probabilities .4/.3/.3. Its alphabet is about 70\% of the leaf count, so duplicate labels occur naturally. The deterministic block compiler maps a leaf to one visible transition, chains sequence children through places, lets XOR children share entry and exit places, and surrounds AND children with a $\tau$ split and join. Its output is called the canonical block representation only to distinguish it from the paired transformation.

\textbf{Isomorphic rename.} The second ordinary-tree net is a clone in which only place and transition identifiers are permuted. Arcs, arc weights, visible labels, and initial/final markings are preserved under a bijection; its language and optimal costs are therefore identical. The perturbation is meaningful because unguided decoding breaks otherwise equal choices by transition-name order.

\textbf{Controlled representation pairs.} Duplicate-prefix nets choose between two enabled transitions labeled $A$, whose markings determine whether $B$ or $C$ follows; silent-routing nets share one $A$ and make that choice through $\tau$ transitions. True concurrency enables $A$ and $B$ together between an invisible split and join, whereas explicit interleaving removes concurrency and encodes $AB$ and $BA$ as separate XOR paths. Finally, the canonical M representation compiles XOR$(B,\mathrm{AND}(A,C))$. In its non-free-choice equivalent, a $\tau$ split marks two places; $A$ consumes the first, $C$ the second, while $B$ requires both. Thus $B$ conflicts with each side despite unequal presets---the characteristic M relation---while the visible complete language remains $B$, $AC$, or $CA$.

Families are assigned to train/validation/test before expansion. Complete visible languages are enumerated and mismatching, unreachable, or dead-transition pairs rejected. Up to 16 clean traces form a pool; two are selected and 20\% remain clean, while the rest receive one to three deletions, insertions, substitutions, repetitions, swaps, or prefix/suffix truncations. Corruption occurs once at label level and is reused across the two representations. The exact backend then aligns and replay-checks all four rows and rejects unequal paired optimal costs. The final corpus contains 2{,}048/512/512 train/validation/test rows; test traces average 3.6 events and optimal costs range from 0 to 7.

\subsection{Exact Targets and Optimization}
\label{sec:training}

Each generated row is labeled by an exact, replay-checked alignment. The target records not only which labels match or are skipped, but which concrete transition the PM4Py optimum fired. When optima are non-unique, identity supervision uses the single witness returned by PM4Py; identity accuracy therefore measures witness agreement rather than correctness. Figure~\ref{fig:training} illustrates how suffix evidence separates two same-label branches. Random label remapping, rather than hashing alone, discourages vocabulary memorization.

The model imitates the exact teacher through
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L} = w_m (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}) + w_c \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cost}} + w_b \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bound}} + w_\ell \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bal}} + w_e \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ent}}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}}$ is cross-entropy over transition identities; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}$ are binary cross-entropies for teacher log and model moves. Only synchronous and model scores are consumed by the current decoder, so $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}}$ is auxiliary. The remaining low-weight terms supervise experimental cost endpoints and router regularization; they never enter decoding, replay, or exact search. Full definitions and weights are provided with the artifact.

Training uses AdamW, random label remapping, and early stopping. The reference run takes about 41 seconds per epoch on one CPU and reaches its best validation loss at epoch 49 of 50 (Figure~\ref{fig:panels}, left). Validation loss is lower than training loss because dropout and label remapping remain active only in the training pass; recognizing which observed events are deviations remains the hardest subtask.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:implementation}

The open-source PyTorch/PM4Py implementation \cite{Berti2023} is available at \url{https://github.com/fit-alessandro-berti/lara-align}, with the checkpoint, result artifacts, and reproduction scripts. Command-line and Python interfaces accept XES/PNML or PM4Py objects and return candidates, replay status, costs, and exact status.

\textbf{Protocol.} Measurements use a 13th-generation Intel Core i7-1355U, CPU-only execution, PyTorch's 10 intra-operation and 10 inter-operation threads, and sequential trace calls. The model is loaded once before timing. Candidate times include feature and per-net runtime construction, the forward pass, decoding, and replay; exact times include PM4Py heuristic initialization and search. Each row is one end-to-end call rather than a repeated warmed microbenchmark, and tables report medians across inputs. Learned metrics use fast mode, so exact repair never contaminates candidate quality or timing; state-equation A* supplies reference optima. We report legality and cost gaps (RQ1), paired guidance and identity diagnostics (RQ2), and representation, scaling, approximation, and real-log results (RQ3).

The accompanying Streamlit workbench runs verified candidates, exact certification, or an exact baseline and keeps candidate, replay, and exact evidence distinct. Screenshots and usage details are provided with the artifact.

\paragraph{Guidance ablation.}
\label{sec:ablation}
Disabling guidance leaves Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} and its marking constraints unchanged but replaces score-driven choices with deterministic transition-name order. These opposite signs do not indicate different behavior---the two nets are isomorphic. They show that the structural name-order baseline happens to be slightly better on the canonical numbering and substantially worse after a semantics-preserving renaming, whereas learned scores remove that identifier sensitivity. The observed canonical reduction, whose interval reaches zero, is therefore as informative as the renamed increase: learning does not dominate a favorable tie-break, but it yields stable quality across the pair.

The controlled motifs delimit this conclusion. Duplicate-prefix guidance does supply a genuine trace-dependent gain, from 68.8\% to 95.3\% ($[17.2,34.4]$), while the structurally determined silent route stays at 98.4\%. Guidance changes no candidate cost for parallel/interleaving or either M representation. Across all pairs it raises representation-cost agreement from 90.2\% to 99.2\%, but this evidence covers seen encodings rather than unseen compilations. Identity is diagnostic rather than certifying: renamed trees match only 63.0\% of teacher transition identities while retaining 84.4\% optimality and perfect paired cost agreement.

Cross-motif rates remain descriptive because ordinary trees are larger and more varied than the controlled motifs. Within-motif pairs are stronger: they share the exact corrupted traces and optimal costs. Establishing which generator class caused a capability would require new training, which this fixed-checkpoint study does not perform. Exact A* also remains 2.3--2.9$\times$ faster in these small cells; the learned path matters only beyond this in-distribution regime.

\paragraph{Failure mechanism.}
\label{sec:failure}
In a representative gap-4 case, $\langle A,D,B,C,C,D\rangle$, the optimum skips the early $D$ and the duplicate $C$. LARA instead inserts model moves to synchronize the early $D$, committing the marking to a branch that forces later skips. The checkpoint produces a log score for this event, but Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} never compares it with the available catch-up path. This case first motivates joint ranking of the log move and catch-up candidates; beam search is the next step if a locally preferred action still causes a poor suffix.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Position among alignment techniques.} The results do not support replacing exact A* or established approximations with the current decoder. Exact A* is faster on small models, and the four tested Petri-net search and compression approximations produce better held-out candidates. LARA contributes something different: one learned proposal mechanism can process unseen labels and nets behind an unchanged semantic contract. Replay establishes a legal upper bound, while exact cost equality or repair establishes optimality. Fast mode returned a legal alignment for every evaluated input, latency stayed below 9 ms on the scaling grid, and it beat exact A* on the tested larger and invisible-heavy receipt models. Certified mode, however, still ran exact A* in every case and therefore has no demonstrated end-to-end speedup; 91.0\% is the fraction retained after exact comparison. Classical methods remain preferable when their quality and coverage fit the workload. LARA is interesting when portability, a predictable verified first result, and exact evidence on demand matter together.

\textbf{What learning contributes now.} The 91.0\% aggregate rate does not mean that every decision needs a neural model. Marking-aware decoding is already strong and cheaper without guidance. The paired ablation isolates two benefits: guidance holds quality at 84.4\% across canonical trees and isomorphic identifier renamings, while the baseline changes, and it raises duplicate-prefix optimality by 26.6 points when future trace context must distinguish equal labels. It changes no candidate cost for the other tested motifs. Learning therefore earns its cost in identifiable cases, while structural, approximate, or exact methods remain better choices elsewhere.

\textbf{Development potential.} The smallest next step is to use the trained log head by ranking a log move against each legal catch-up candidate. A beam can then retain several markings when local ranking is insufficient. Neural-guided A* can use learned scores only for queue ordering while an admissible heuristic retains proof authority, and the replay-verified cost can become an incumbent upper bound that prunes exact work. The same certifier can compare learned and classical candidates, while a portfolio selects among unguided, learned, approximate, and exact methods. Batching can amortize inference across variants. These changes improve proposal or scheduling quality without changing legality or certification.

\textbf{Limitations and validity.} On the largest stress models only 30 to 44\% of candidates are optimal, classical approximations are much closer to the optimum, and inference is needless overhead on simple inputs. Cost intervals are uncalibrated, costs are unitary, and real-log evidence covers two logs. All tested representation pairs occur in training, scaling cells contain 10 samples, and the PM4Py configurations are not an exhaustive tuning study. Paired evaluation, independent replay, and exact references strengthen the comparison, but broader logs, model classes, costs, lifecycle attributes, and representation shifts are needed before claiming wider generality. Transition-identity agreement remains diagnostic because several optimal alignments may coexist.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

LARA shows that reusable learned guidance can assist alignment without becoming its authority. Its current value is a verified candidate architecture: replay accepted every evaluated output, 91.0\% of held-out candidates were already optimal, guidance improved representation stability and duplicate-label decisions, and the same checkpoint processed unseen labels and discovered nets. Exact A* still runs for every certified result, and strong approximations produce better candidates, so LARA is neither a certified-mode speedup nor the new default decoder.

Its potential follows from separating proposal from proof. Joint log and catch-up ranking, beams, learned queue ordering, incumbent pruning, classical candidates, portfolios, and batched inference can improve quality or total work before the verifier, while replay and exact cost retain final authority. This gives a concrete path toward progressive alignment services that return a safe proposal immediately and exact evidence when required.
