\begin{abstract}
Alignments explain, event by event, how a trace deviates from a process model, but computing an optimal alignment requires exact shortest-path search, often the bottleneck on real discovered models. Purely learned aligners are fast but may return illegal or silently suboptimal alignments. LARA (Learned Adaptive Recombined Alignments) combines both: a small neural foundation model, trained once on synthetic data and applicable to any net and any log without retraining, scores alignment moves; a constrained decoder turns the scores into a candidate; exact replay verifies it; and an exact backend certifies or repairs it on demand. Every answer is therefore a legal alignment with a known cost, and optionally a certified optimal one. In the assessment, all produced alignments are legal and 91\% attain the exact optimal cost. An ablation attributes the learned contribution specifically to the resolution of duplicate labels. Legality transfers zero-shot to stress nets and real-life logs, and the fast path overtakes exact search on larger nets and on real discovered models dominated by invisible transitions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining relates event data to process models \cite{Aalst2016}; conformance checking compares their behavior, and alignments explain each event through synchronous, log, and model moves \cite{Adriansyah2011}. An optimal alignment minimizes deviation cost, yielding both a fitness value and a diagnostic account of disagreement. Computing it requires shortest-path search over a synchronous product, commonly guided by marking-equation heuristics \cite{Dongen2018,Zelst2018}. The state space grows with concurrency, loops, duplicate labels, and invisible transitions, making exact alignment intrinsically hard \cite{Schwanen2026}. These constructs are common in discovered models, so one difficult variant can dominate log-level running time and make interactive analysis unpredictable.

Learned guidance can provide low-latency candidates, but a prediction may be illegal or silently suboptimal. Either failure is consequential: an illegal sequence is not an explanation of the observed case, while an unreported cost gap distorts fitness and downstream diagnostics. LARA (Learned Adaptive Recombined Alignments) therefore enforces one invariant: \emph{neural output is never a certificate}. A neural model scores moves; a marking-aware greedy decoder constructs a candidate; replay verifies legality and cost; and an exact backend certifies it by cost equality or repairs it. The same pipeline supports a fast verified upper bound, a fully certified result, and an anytime interval. Thus learning proposes, while process semantics supplies every guarantee.

The neural component is a small alignment \emph{foundation model}: trained once on exact-labeled synthetic data, it uses hashed labels rather than a fixed vocabulary and transfers to new logs and Petri nets without retraining. Its latency is dominated by feature extraction and one forward pass, while exact search may be heavy-tailed. The contributions are (1) this reusable, topology- and trace-conditioned move scorer; (2) a certifying architecture that isolates learned proposal from exact trust (Section~\ref{sec:approach}); (3) an exact-labeled synthetic pipeline that supervises transition identities rather than labels alone; and (4) an assessment that attributes quality to learning, locates the scaling threshold, compares established approximations, and tests zero-shot transfer (Section~\ref{sec:assessment}). The claim is deliberately narrower than neural replacement of exact alignment: LARA is a certifiable candidate generator whose present decoder may still be repaired.

We ask: \textbf{RQ1}, are candidates legal on unseen nets and vocabularies? \textbf{RQ2}, how often do they attain optimal cost, and with what gaps otherwise? \textbf{RQ3}, are duplicate labels and invisible routing resolved? \textbf{RQ4}, do results transfer across families, scales, and real logs? \textbf{RQ5}, how often does cost equality certify the candidate? \textbf{RQ6}, what quality comes from learned scores rather than constrained decoding?

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:relatedWork} positions LARA in the literature, Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} establishes the formal setting, Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents the architecture and training procedure, Section~\ref{sec:assessment} reports the assessment, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the implications and conclude.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}

\textbf{Exact and decomposed alignment.} Alignments are shortest paths through a synchronous product \cite{Zelst2018}; marking-equation heuristics provide admissible lower bounds and accelerate exact search \cite{Dongen2018}, but worst-case complexity remains high \cite{Schwanen2026}. Structural decomposition reduces the state space by aligning fragments, after which recomposition restores a global result or bound \cite{Aalst2013,Lee2018}. Both families derive their guarantees from the model. LARA retains that principle but changes the division of labor: its neural scorer predicts trace-dependent latent regions and move preferences, while replay and exact search alone establish claims about the returned alignment. The regions are diagnostic predictions, not a structural decomposition theorem.

\textbf{Approximation.} Discounted A* changes the cost function \cite{Boltenhagen2021}; fixed-horizon search commits bounded prefixes \cite{Dongen2017}; tandem repeats compress traces \cite{ReissnerTandem2020}; sliding windows retain promising boundary markings \cite{Bogdanov2024}; and subset selection transfers representative alignments by edit distance \cite{FaniSani2020}. These methods exploit different regularities and therefore have different failure surfaces: bounded prefixes and windows lose global context, compression depends on repetition, and subset transfer depends on representative traces. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares their PM4Py implementations under independent replay and common unit costs. LARA differs less in output format than in trust handling: it verifies every candidate, exposes its cost as an upper bound, and lets cost equality certify it or exact search repair it.

\textbf{Learning.} Neural process models largely target prediction \cite{Bukhsh2021}, and a recent survey identifies conformance checking as an open AI opportunity \cite{Genga2025}. Predicting the next activity or a scalar fitness value does not provide the move-by-move explanation required here. LARA instead constructs an alignment against an explicit Petri net and retains concrete transition identities. Its learned scores guide a constrained decoder but never act as admissible heuristics or certificates. This conservative interface makes distribution shift observable as a larger candidate gap or an exact repair, rather than silently weakening the semantics of conformance checking.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

\textbf{Traces and nets.} Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite activity alphabet and $\sigma = \langle a_1,\ldots,a_n\rangle\in\mathcal{A}^*$ a trace; a log is a multiset of traces. A Petri net is $N=(P,T,F)$, where $P$ and $T$ are disjoint sets of places and transitions and $F\subseteq(P\times T)\cup(T\times P)$ is the flow relation. A marking $m:P\to\mathbb{N}$ assigns tokens to places. Transition $t$ is enabled when its input places contain the required tokens; firing consumes these tokens and produces tokens in its output places, written $m\xrightarrow{t}m'$. The labeling $\lambda:T\to\mathcal{A}\cup\{\tau\}$ permits invisible routing ($\tau$) and duplicate visible labels. These constructs matter because an event identifies a matching label but not necessarily a transition identity. A system net $SN=(N,m_0,m_f)$ adds initial and final markings.

\textbf{Alignments and cost.} With skip symbol $\gg$, a move is synchronous $(a,t)$ when $\lambda(t)=a$, log $(a,\gg)$, or model $(\gg,t)$; $(\gg,\gg)$ is excluded. An alignment $\gamma$ is \emph{legal} iff (1) its non-skip log projection equals $\sigma$ exactly and (2) its model projection is a firing sequence from $m_0$ to $m_f$. Moves retain transition identities, so equal label projections may still choose different duplicate transitions and induce different future behavior. Under unit costs, synchronous and invisible model moves cost $0$, while log and visible model moves cost $1$. Thus
\[
  \delta(\sigma,SN)=\min\{c(\gamma)\mid \gamma\text{ is legal for }\sigma\text{ and }SN\}
\]
is the minimum number of visible deviations required to explain the trace. Optimal alignments need not be unique, but their cost is.

\textbf{Exact search and certification.} The classical construction combines $SN$ with a linear trace net. Legal alignments correspond to complete firing sequences of this synchronous product, making optimal alignment a shortest-path problem \cite{Zelst2018}; A* uses admissible lower bounds derived from the marking equation \cite{Dongen2018}. We use PM4Py's state-equation A* aligner \cite{Berti2023} as exact backend and teacher. For a replay-verified candidate, equality with the exact cost $\delta$ certifies optimality even when the move sequence differs. A lower bound that meets the candidate cost could certify it earlier; otherwise, the exact solver supplies $\delta$ and an exact alignment for repair.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

LARA separates learned, fast candidate generation from exact, trusted certification. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} follows a net--trace pair through encoding, neural move scoring, marking-aware decoding, replay, and exact certification. Training is offline and amortized over later use.


\subsection{Input Encoding: Reading the Net and the Trace}
\label{sec:encoding}

\emph{Input:} $SN=(N,m_0,m_f)$ and trace $\sigma$ of length $n$. \emph{Output:} a typed net graph, hashed trace labels, and compatibility mask $C$.

\textbf{Typed graph.} The net becomes a bipartite graph with one node per place and transition. Places carry initial/final token counts and degrees, exposing where execution starts and ends and where branches synchronize. Transitions carry invisibility, move costs, degrees, and a self-loop flag, exposing routing, choice, and repeatable behavior. Each arc becomes two typed directed edges: the type distinguishes presets, which enable a transition, from postsets, which receive its tokens, while the reverse edge lets downstream context flow back toward a choice. This is essential for duplicate labels. If two transitions have symmetric presets but different suffixes, forward-only messages leave them indistinguishable; bidirectional messages bring that suffix evidence to each duplicate. Encoding the net itself keeps the input linear in net size and avoids materializing its potentially exponential reachability graph.

\textbf{Vocabulary-free labels.} Stable BLAKE2b hashing maps labels to 8{,}192 identifiers, reserving one for $\tau$. Equal event and transition labels share an identifier, so they begin from the same embedding without requiring a global activity dictionary. The trace is therefore portable across organizations and naming conventions, and a checkpoint can process unseen vocabularies without changing its output layer. The compatibility mask has $C_{ij}=1$ exactly when event $i$ and transition $j$ carry the same visible label. Hashing prevents activity-name memorization, while the hard mask—not a learned preference—ensures that every proposed synchronous move remains label-consistent.

\subsection{Neural Scoring: Three Questions per Net and Trace Pair}
\label{sec:architecture}

\emph{Input:} the encoded pair. \emph{Output:} synchronous scores $\mathrm{sync}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|T|}$ (masked by $C$), log-move scores in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and model-move scores in $\mathbb{R}^{|T|}$. These answer which concrete transition explains each event, which events should be skipped, and which unmatched transitions the model requires. The model has about 1.7M parameters ($d=96$) and runs on CPU.

\textbf{Coupled encoders.} A two-layer graph transformer exchanges typed messages on the net, combining global self-attention with local arc-direction-specific updates. A sequence transformer \cite{Vaswani2017} reads the trace, adds positions, and uses the same label embeddings as the transition nodes. Bidirectional cross-attention then lets events inspect the transitions that could explain them and lets transitions inspect the events that could justify firing them. Consequently, the same net receives different move scores for different traces: the representation is structural, but the decisions are trace-dependent.

\textbf{Scoring heads.} A scaled bilinear head scores event--transition pairs and applies $C$ as a hard mask. A row therefore represents a distribution over concrete transition identities, not merely activity labels, and can resolve duplicates that lead to different suffixes. Linear heads read log-move scores from event representations and model-move scores from transition representations. These three outputs mirror the three move types of an alignment and are the only neural quantities consumed by the decoder.

\textbf{Diagnostic decomposition.} A router softly assigns events and transitions to $R=6$ latent regions. Each local expert predicts one of four deviation sketches, lower and upper cost estimates, and uncertainty; summing regional upper estimates yields a trace-level cost prediction (Figure~\ref{fig:regions}). The branch answers a different question from the move heads: not which move to execute next, but where deviation evidence lies and how much it may cost. Unlike structural decomposition \cite{Aalst2013}, regions depend jointly on the net and trace and need not be semantically independent. These learned bounds are diagnostic only and never guide decoding or exact search because they are not admissible.

\subsection{Decoding, Verification, and Certification: From Scores to a Certified Alignment}
\label{sec:decoding}

\emph{Input:} the net, trace, and move scores. \emph{Output:} a replay-verified upper bound, a certified optimum, or an exact repair. Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} gives the procedure and Figure~\ref{fig:decoding} an example. Scores affect only decoding; replay and exact search inspect the resulting alignment, never neural output.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Guided greedy decoding, verification, and certification.}
\label{alg:decode}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require system net $SN = (N, m_0, m_f)$, trace $\sigma = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_n \rangle$, move scores of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, mode
\State $\gamma \gets \langle\,\rangle$;\quad $m \gets m_0$ \Comment{every appended model component is fired on $m$}
\For{$i = 1, \ldots, n$}
  \State $E \gets \{\, t \text{ enabled at } m \mid \lambda(t) = a_i \,\}$
  \If{$|E| = 1$} append the synchronous move $(a_i, t)$ \Comment{forced by the marking}
  \ElsIf{$|E| > 1$} append $(a_i, t^\ast)$ with $t^\ast = \arg\max_{t \in E} \mathrm{sync}(i, t)$ \Comment{duplicate labels}
  \Else{} BFS over model moves from $m$, depth $\leq 8$: invisible transitions first, then by model-move score
    \If{the search reaches a marking enabling $a_i$} append its model moves, then the synchronous move
    \Else{} append the log move $(a_i, \gg)$ \Comment{the net cannot catch up}
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State append model moves reaching $m_f$ (bounded search, depth $\leq 32$)
\State \emph{verify}: replay $\gamma$ from $m_0$; check that $m_f$ is reached and the log projection equals $\sigma$; compute $c(\gamma)$
\If{mode is \textsc{fast}} \Return $\gamma$ with the verified upper bound $c(\gamma) \geq \delta(\sigma, SN)$
\Else{} compute $\delta$ by exact A*; \Return $\gamma$ certified if $c(\gamma) = \delta$, else the exact alignment as repair
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The decoder walks over the trace once and always knows the current marking, so every appended model component is fireable. It delegates nothing that process semantics can decide. A unique enabled label match synchronizes without consulting the network; if no short model-move path can enable the event, the decoder emits a log move. Learned scores settle only the discretionary choices. Synchronous scores select among several enabled transitions with the same label, where the trace suffix can distinguish branches that a structural tie-break cannot. Model-move scores order alternative catch-up routes through invisible or visible transitions. The diagnostic regions and predicted costs never participate in these decisions.

This design differs fundamentally from exact alignment. The bounded searches explore model moves only, stop at the first enabling marking rather than the cheapest globally compatible marking, and never revise an earlier commitment. They guarantee replayability, not low cost: replay independently checks both projections, establishes legality, and computes $c(\gamma)$. \textsc{Fast} returns that verified upper bound. \textsc{Certified} invokes exact A* and either certifies the candidate by $c(\gamma)=\delta$ or returns the exact alignment as repair. \textsc{Anytime} exposes the verified upper bound together with the best exact lower bound when the backend times out. A precomputed per-net runtime keeps local searches cheap, so fast-mode latency is dominated by encoding and the forward pass. The irrevocable commitments, rather than an inability to represent the correct move, explain the failures in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\subsection{Training Data Generation}
\label{sec:data}

The sampling unit is a \emph{behavior family}: one visible behavior, two Petri-net representations, and two observed traces shared by both nets, hence four rows. Table~\ref{tab:motifs} defines the four equally weighted motifs. The three controlled motifs receive the same two fresh sequential activities after the displayed core, making traces less degenerate without changing the paired comparison.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Training motifs and their two representations. Core languages omit the shared two-activity suffix. ``Canonical'' denotes this generator's fixed block compiler, not a universal Petri-net normal form.}
\label{tab:motifs}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3.5pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.23\linewidth}p{0.54\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
motif & core complete traces & representation pair \\
\midrule
ordinary tree & sampled process-tree language & canonical block compilation / isomorphic identifier rename \\
duplicate / silent & $\langle A,B\rangle$, $\langle A,C\rangle$ & two concrete $A$ transitions leading to $B$ or $C$ / one $A$ followed by a $\tau$ choice \\
concurrency & $\langle A,B\rangle$, $\langle B,A\rangle$ & true AND split--join / explicit XOR of the sequences $AB$ and $BA$ \\
M-pattern & $\langle B\rangle$, $\langle A,C\rangle$, $\langle C,A\rangle$ & block XOR$(B,\mathrm{AND}(A,C))$ / non-free-choice M-net \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Ordinary tree and canonical block.} ``Ordinary'' names the heterogeneous random class, not a Petri net whose graph is a tree. A process tree has 4--12 visible leaves, depth at most six, and sequence/XOR/AND operators sampled with probabilities .4/.3/.3. Its alphabet is about 70\% of the leaf count, so duplicate labels occur naturally. The deterministic block compiler maps a leaf to one visible transition, chains sequence children through places, lets XOR children share entry and exit places, and surrounds AND children with a $\tau$ split and join. Its output is called the canonical block representation only to distinguish it from the paired transformation.

\textbf{Isomorphic rename.} The second ordinary-tree net is a clone in which only place and transition identifiers are permuted. Arcs, arc weights, visible labels, and initial/final markings are preserved under a bijection; its language and optimal costs are therefore identical. The perturbation is meaningful because unguided decoding breaks otherwise equal choices by transition-name order.

\textbf{Controlled representation pairs.} Duplicate-prefix nets choose between two enabled transitions labeled $A$, whose markings determine whether $B$ or $C$ follows; silent-routing nets share one $A$ and make that choice through $\tau$ transitions. True concurrency enables $A$ and $B$ together between an invisible split and join, whereas explicit interleaving removes concurrency and encodes $AB$ and $BA$ as separate XOR paths. Finally, the canonical M representation compiles XOR$(B,\mathrm{AND}(A,C))$. In its non-free-choice equivalent, a $\tau$ split marks two places; $A$ consumes the first, $C$ the second, while $B$ requires both. Thus $B$ conflicts with each side despite unequal presets---the characteristic M relation---while the visible complete language remains $B$, $AC$, or $CA$.

Families are assigned to train/validation/test before expansion. Complete visible languages are enumerated and mismatching, unreachable, or dead-transition pairs rejected. Up to 16 clean traces form a pool; two are selected and 20\% remain clean, while the rest receive one to three deletions, insertions, substitutions, repetitions, swaps, or prefix/suffix truncations. Corruption occurs once at label level and is reused across the two representations. The exact backend then aligns and replay-checks all four rows and rejects unequal paired optimal costs. The final corpus contains 2{,}048/512/512 train/validation/test rows; test traces average 3.6 events and optimal costs range from 0 to 7.

\subsection{Exact Targets and Optimization}
\label{sec:training}

Each generated row is labeled by an exact, replay-checked alignment. The target records not only which labels match or are skipped, but which concrete transition the optimum fired. That identity supervision makes duplicate-label resolution learnable; Figure~\ref{fig:training} illustrates how suffix evidence separates two same-label branches. Hashed labels prevent the model from solving the task by memorizing a vocabulary.

The model imitates the exact teacher through
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L} = w_m (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}) + w_c \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cost}} + w_b \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bound}} + w_\ell \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bal}} + w_e \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ent}}.
\end{equation*}
The first three terms answer the alignment questions of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sync}}$ is cross-entropy over transition identities; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{log}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{model}}$ are binary cross-entropies for events skipped by the optimum and transitions fired without an event. The diagnostic losses have a separate role. Smooth-L1 cost loss supervises the sum of regional upper estimates; boundary loss discourages adjacent fragments from splitting arbitrarily, balance loss prevents one region from absorbing the net, and entropy loss encourages decisive assignments. Weights ($w_m=1$, $w_c=0.03$, $w_b=w_\ell=0.01$, $w_e=0.001$) keep move imitation dominant, so the decomposition remains explanatory rather than a hidden search heuristic.

Training uses AdamW, random label remapping, and early stopping. The reference run takes about 41 seconds per epoch on one CPU and reaches its best validation loss at epoch 49 of 50 (Figure~\ref{fig:panels}, left). Validation loss is lower than training loss because dropout and label remapping remain active only in the training pass; recognizing which observed events are deviations remains the hardest subtask.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:implementation}

The open-source PyTorch/PM4Py implementation \cite{Berti2023} is available at \url{https://github.com/fit-alessandro-berti/lara-align}, with the checkpoint, result artifacts, and reproduction scripts. Command-line and Python interfaces accept XES/PNML or PM4Py objects and return alignments, costs, bounds, and certification status.

\textbf{Protocol.} All runs use one CPU. Learned metrics use fast mode only, so exact repair never contaminates candidate quality or timing; PM4Py state-equation A* supplies the optimum for ground truth. We report replay legality (RQ1), exact-cost rate and $c(\gamma)-\delta$ (RQ2), transition-identity and label-level matches (RQ3), family, scaling, and real-log transfer (RQ4), cost-equality certification (RQ5), and a paired guidance ablation (RQ6).

The cost-gap distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:panels}, right) is concentrated at zero: 466 of 512 candidates are optimal, 95\% lie within two cost units, and no gap exceeds four. The lower sequence-match rates do not contradict optimality: several legal move sequences can have the same minimum cost, especially with silent transitions and duplicate labels. \paragraph{Guidance ablation.}
\label{sec:ablation}
Disabling guidance leaves Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} and its marking constraints unchanged but replaces score-driven choices with deterministic transition-name order. Invisible transitions still precede visible ones; only ordering inside each group changes. The ablation therefore isolates trace-dependent ranking from the strong legality prior. Guidance adds 4.1 optimality points overall (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), but the sign and size depend on representation; Section~\ref{sec:classes} analyzes this rather than treating the duplicate-prefix gain as universal (RQ3, RQ6).

\paragraph{Learned cost estimate.}
\label{sec:costest}
The diagnostic estimate tracks exact cost ($r=0.82$) and separates fitting from deviating traces: thresholding it at 0.5 detects any deviation with 89.8\% accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:costest}). It is therefore useful for prioritizing traces before alignment. Its intervals are not calibrated, however: only 29.1\% contain the optimum. This empirical distinction between a useful point estimate and an unsafe bound justifies keeping the diagnostic branch outside both decoding and exact search.

The ordinary-tree pair is the most revealing attribution result. Guided decoding is 84.4\% optimal on both the canonical net and its isomorphic rename, with identical candidate costs on all 64 paired traces. Within-motif pairs are stronger: they share the exact corrupted traces and optimal costs. Establishing which generator class caused a capability would require new training, which this fixed-checkpoint study does not perform. Exact A* also remains 2.3--2.9$\times$ faster in these small cells; the learned path matters only beyond this in-distribution regime.

\paragraph{Failure mechanism.}
\label{sec:failure}
In a representative gap-4 case, $\langle A,D,B,C,C,D\rangle$, the optimum skips the early $D$ and the duplicate $C$. LARA instead inserts model moves to synchronize the early $D$, committing the marking to a branch that forces later skips. The suffix-aware representation contains the recurrence needed to prefer the optimum, but Algorithm~\ref{alg:decode} optimizes one decision at a time and cannot revisit the commitment. This concrete trace supports the scaling evidence: search over the scores, rather than a larger encoder alone, is the likely route to better quality.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Implications.} Separating proposal from trust preserves legality under distribution shift and turns poor candidates into visible repairs rather than silent errors. This is more than a defensive implementation detail. It permits one checkpoint to be used across logs while assurance remains selectable per workload: exploratory dashboards may consume verified upper bounds immediately, whereas audit-facing analyses can wait for exact cost equality or repair. Neural and exact components can consequently improve independently without changing what a certificate means.

The ablation also changes how accuracy should be read. Constrained decoding is already strong, and its transition-name tie-break can be accidentally favorable: guidance is observed 1.6 points lower on canonical ordinary trees but 7.8 points higher after an isomorphic identifier rename, producing identical 84.4\% guided quality on both. Learning therefore contributes representation stability rather than uniform improvement. Its separate 26.6-point duplicate-prefix gain identifies the residual case where trace context, not naming or marking constraints, resolves the choice. Reporting only 91.0\% aggregate optimality would obscure both roles.

\textbf{Limitations.} At 40 activities only 30--44\% of candidates are optimal, and strong PM4Py approximations are closer to optimum. The current model also pays a roughly 5 ms forward-pass floor even when a simple exact or unguided method would finish sooner. LARA is therefore not a universal replacement for exact A* or for established approximations: its useful regime combines costly exact search with a tolerance for verified, possibly non-optimal candidates or a need for predictable first results.

Training on larger stress nets may improve representations, but the ablation, failure trace, and approximation comparison point chiefly to irrevocable decoding. A beam could preserve several markings through an ambiguous prefix; neural-guided A* could use scores for ordering while retaining an admissible heuristic for proof; a portfolio could select between exact, classical approximate, and learned candidates from net and trace features. Larger logs, GPU batching, non-unit costs, lifecycle attributes, and richer model classes remain open.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} Internal validity is strengthened by paired guided and unguided runs on identical inputs, independent replay of every candidate, and quality metrics computed against exact optima. Nonetheless, synthetic generators determine which ambiguities are frequent, and hyperparameters were selected on the same broad family of generated behavior. The approximation comparison uses concrete PM4Py configurations rather than an exhaustive tuning study, so rankings should be interpreted for those implementations and limits.

The scaling cells contain only 10 samples and one exact timeout; they locate a crossover trend rather than estimate population runtimes precisely. Two public logs cannot establish external generality, although the zero-shot protocol is strict: neither labels nor discovered nets are adapted to the checkpoint. Finally, cost equality gives strong construct validity for certification, but transition-sequence agreement with one exact alignment is only diagnostic because several optimal alignments can coexist.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

LARA combines a vocabulary-free move scorer with replay verification and exact certification. It returns legal alignments throughout the study, certifies 91.0\% in distribution, raises paired representation-cost agreement from 90.2\% to 99.2\%, improves duplicate-prefix optimality from 68.8\% to 95.3\%, and overtakes exact search on larger or invisible-heavy models. Strong PM4Py approximations produce better candidates, however, so the contribution is the certifying learned architecture rather than a dominant decoder; improving search over its scores is the clearest next step.
